Build Your Own NES Emulator

Milestone: a game, start to finish

Nineteen chapters of building. This one builds nothing at all. Today we go shopping — because the real test of a console isn’t the games it was built alongside; it’s the game nobody prepared it for.

The homebrew shelf

Shiru’s page — the same place Chase and Lan Master came from — holds a whole shelf of NES games, free to download, source and manuals included. Time to test the console the only way that counts: with a game nobody prepared it for.

Now the experiment. Zooming Secretary: a game no chapter of this book has ever mentioned, downloaded fresh, straight into your emulator:

cargo run roms/Zooming_Secretary.nes

The logo comes up. Press Enter. An office: two floors, ladders, desks, filing cabinets, a clock on the wall — and a scoreboard reading DAY 1, CALL 00/10, MISS 0/3. Phones ring, music plays, and a small determined secretary answers to your arrow keys. Nobody patched anything. No chapter secretly prepared for this game. It simply runs, because the machine under it is no longer a Chase-player — it’s a console. (The manual in the zip explains the phones; you’ll be fired three misses at a time until you read it.)

Alter Ego runs too — credits, a starfield title, and a puzzle game about a hero and his mirror-image ghost. Its first level has a name, printed across the top of the screen:

The fourth game this machine has ever met says hello. (Alter Ego: game by Denis Grachev, NES conversion by Shiru, music by kulor.)

The whole machine

What is it, exactly, that runs them? This:

The diagram is also a map of the book — every box names the chapters where you typed it into being. And a frame of any game is one lap around it: the CPU spends 27,393 cycles computing through the bus, the NMI taps, the game hoses its sprites through $4014 and its notes through $4000, and the window and speaker get their sixtieth of a second. There is nothing else. That’s the console.

Everything that is still wrong

The shelf also keeps score. Lawn Mower — same author, same year, plain NROM — boots to a black screen. Freeze the machine and look where it’s stuck:

CE13  2C 02 20  BIT $2002
CE16  50 FB     BVC $CE13

It reads PPUSTATUS four thousand times a frame, waiting for bit 6 — a flag our PPU has never once raised. That’s the sprite-zero hit, the mid-frame signal games use to split a scrolling field from a fixed scoreboard; and its title doesn’t fade in, it slides in, on scrolling registers we silently swallow. Two missing pieces, one dark screen — and both have chapter numbers. The full ledger, every entry labeled the day it was skipped:

  • Scrolling — $2005 ignored since chapter 15; Chase’s eight-pixel nudge, Lawn Mower’s sliding title, and every side-scroller ever made. Chapter 24.
  • Sprite zero and friends — the hit flag, the eight-per-scanline limit, the overflow bit. Chapter 25.
  • Mid-frame tricks — split screens, status bars, PPUMASK honored instead of shrugged at. Chapters 26, 21.
  • The dot-by-dot clock — our frame is 29,780 cycles; the truth is 29,780½, and the PPU is a machine of dots, not scanlines-at-a-rest. PAL exists too. Chapters 22–23.
  • Interrupt timing, exactly — the NMI to the dot, IRQs at all. Chapter 27.
  • Bigger cartridges — every mapper that isn’t NROM, which is most games you’ve heard of. Chapters 28–29.
  • The rest of the orchestra — triangle bass, noise drums, the real frame counter and envelopes. Chapter 30. The sample channel, and the DMA that steals CPU cycles and controller bits while it plays — plus OAM DMA’s unpaid 513. Chapter 31.
  • Ghosts — open bus, and the behaviors exactly one game depends on. Chapter 32.

Where you are on the map

Chapter 2 put a gradient in a window and called it a triumph. Between there and here you built a CPU checked against a real 6502’s diary down to the cycle, a picture chip whose frames match a mature emulator byte for byte, a controller, a clock, and half a sound chip — and a stranger’s game just ran on the pile, first try.

Part II is called Make It Right. It begins by doing something rude: running ROMs that break this emulator on purpose, so every simplification we labeled gets caught in the act instead of taken on faith. Then it rebuilds the picture a dot at a time, until the ledger above is paid off line by line — Lawn Mower’s screen lights up, worlds scroll, scoreboards hold still while everything under them moves, and the orchestra gets its missing sections. The machine works. Next, we make it true.

Try it

  • There’s one more zip on the shelf small enough for tonight: ny2011, Shiru’s New Year card. Snowy trees and a carol, on your emulator. Save it for December 31st if you have the discipline.
  • Survive to DAY 3 of Zooming Secretary. The phones do not get nicer.
  • Read the manuals in the zips — they’re lovely, hand-made things, and Lawn Mower’s will show you exactly what you’re missing until chapter 25.
  • Go hunting: any homebrew ROM whose header says mapper 0 is already yours. You know how to check byte 6 — you wrote the parser.

For the curious

The zips hide a treat: Shiru’s development notes for every game. Lawn Mower’s complain that a palette tuned in one emulator looked wrong in every other — “one color can vary from lime to clean yellow” — which is the same lesson our twin whites taught in chapter 18, seen from the game author’s side of the glass; Part II computes colors from the television signal itself to end the argument. And Zooming Secretary’s notes reveal it almost didn’t fit in NROM: they saved the last kilobytes by dropping the sample channel and rewriting C i++ as ++i. The ceiling you built this console up to is a ceiling its games were carved to fit.
